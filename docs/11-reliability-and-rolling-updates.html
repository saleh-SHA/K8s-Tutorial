Chapter 11

Reliability & rolling updates

Tell Kubernetes how to tell when your application is healthy.

The three probes

Kubernetes can only keep your application healthy if you tell it how to check. There are three kinds of probes.

spec:
  containers:
  - name: app
    image: myapp:1.0
    ports:
    - containerPort: 8080
    startupProbe:
      httpGet:
        path: /healthz
        port: 8080
      failureThreshold: 30
      periodSeconds: 10
    livenessProbe:
      httpGet:
        path: /healthz
        port: 8080
      periodSeconds: 10
    readinessProbe:
      httpGet:
        path: /ready
        port: 8080
      periodSeconds: 5

Liveness vs readiness, in practice

A liveness failure kills and restarts the container. A readiness failure pulls it out of Service rotation but does not restart it. Use readiness for transient conditions like "waiting for a database connection to recover." Use liveness only for deadlock-style situations where a restart is genuinely the only fix. An overly aggressive liveness probe is one of the most common ways teams accidentally cause cascading failures.

Rolling updates

When you change a Deployment's image, two ReplicaSets briefly coexist, and traffic shifts gradually.

spec:
  strategy:
    type: RollingUpdate
    rollingUpdate:
      maxSurge: 25%
      maxUnavailable: 0

maxUnavailable: 0 means never drop below the desired replica count during a rollout, at the cost of needing extra capacity during the rollout itself. If a rollout goes wrong, roll back:

kubectl rollout history deployment/web
kubectl rollout undo deployment/web

Pod Disruption Budgets

A PodDisruptionBudget guards against voluntary disruptions such as node drains for maintenance. Setting minAvailable: 2 on a Deployment with three replicas tells Kubernetes that at most one pod can be voluntarily disrupted at a time.

apiVersion: policy/v1
kind: PodDisruptionBudget
metadata:
  name: web
spec:
  minAvailable: 2
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: web

Measuring reliability: SLIs and SLOs

"Is the service healthy?" is too vague to act on. Reliability engineering makes it precise with two terms. An SLI (Service Level Indicator) is a measured number — the actual value of something users care about. An SLO (Service Level Objective) is the target you hold that indicator to. "99.9% of requests succeed over 30 days" is an SLO; the measured success rate is its SLI.

Four indicators ("the golden signals") cover most services: availability (the fraction of requests that succeed), latency (how long requests take — track a percentile like p99, not an average, because averages hide the slow tail), error rate (the fraction returning failures), and throughput (requests per second). For the shop you might set: API availability ≥ 99.9%, p99 latency < 300 ms, error rate < 0.1%.

SLOs turn reliability into a budget you can spend. If your target is 99.9% availability, you have an error budget of 0.1% — roughly 43 minutes of downtime a month. That budget makes decisions concrete: budget to spare → ship features faster; budget exhausted → freeze risky changes and shore up reliability. The probes earlier in this chapter feed these numbers: a readiness probe pulling an unhealthy pod from rotation is the difference between a failed request (spends budget) and a retried one (does not).

Failure domains: node, zone, region

Replicas only help if a single failure cannot take them all down at once. Failures cluster at three levels: a node (one machine dies), a zone (a whole datacenter/availability zone loses power or network), and a region (a geographic area goes down — rare but real). Three replicas packed onto one node survive a process crash but not a node failure; three replicas in one zone survive a node failure but not a zone outage. Matching your redundancy to the failure domain you need to survive is the core of high-availability design.

Spreading pods with anti-affinity and topology spread

By default the scheduler may place all of a Deployment's replicas on the same node, which quietly defeats the point of having replicas. Two mechanisms prevent that. Pod anti-affinity tells the scheduler to keep pods of the same app apart; topology spread constraints (the newer, more expressive option) ask for an even distribution across a topology key such as node or zone.

spec:
  template:
    spec:
      topologySpreadConstraints:
      - maxSkew: 1                              # replicas differ by at most 1...
        topologyKey: topology.kubernetes.io/zone   # ...across zones
        whenUnsatisfiable: DoNotSchedule
        labelSelector:
          matchLabels:
            app: web
      - maxSkew: 1
        topologyKey: kubernetes.io/hostname      # ...and across nodes
        whenUnsatisfiable: ScheduleAnyway        # soft: prefer, don't block

With this, the shop's three web replicas land on three different nodes across availability zones. A node failure now costs one replica out of three, not the whole service — and combined with a PodDisruptionBudget, even planned maintenance keeps the service up. DoNotSchedule makes the constraint hard (the pod stays Pending rather than violate it); ScheduleAnyway makes it a strong preference. Use hard constraints for zone spread on critical services, soft ones where capacity is tight.
